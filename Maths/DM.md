### 2. Applying Your "Ask" Framework

Your questions are excellent for **Mathematical Modeling**. When you encounter a problem, use your list as a checklist:

*   **"What are the objects?"** (Identify your **Sets**).
*   **"What is the state?"** (Define the current **Tuple** or configuration of variables).
*   **"What are the possible states?"** (Define the **State Space** or the **Power Set**).
*   **"What connects one state to another?"** (Define the **Relation** or **Function**).
*   **"Can I represent this as a graph?"** (Nodes = states, Edges = transitions).
*   **"Can I group these objects into equivalence classes?"** (Use **Equivalence Relations** to ignore irrelevant differences and simplify the problem).
*   **"Is there a smaller representation of the state?"** (Look for a **Partition** or a more efficient **Mapping**).

### 3. Recommended Resources

#### The "Gold Standard" Textbooks
*   **"Discrete Mathematics and Its Applications" by Kenneth Rosen:** The comprehensive industry standard. It covers every single item on your list in depth with plenty of exercises.
*   **"Discrete Mathematics with Applications" by Susanna Epp:** Known for being more accessible and explaining the logic behind the proofs more clearly than Rosen.

#### Online Courses (Free)
*   **MIT OpenCourseWare: Mathematics for Computer Science (6.042J):** This is perhaps the best free resource available. It covers sets, relations, graphs, and state machines. [Link to MIT OCW](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/6-042j-mathematics-for-computer-science-fall-2010/)
*   **Coursera: Introduction to Discrete Mathematics for Computer Science Specialization:** Offered by UC San Diego. It is broken into small, digestible courses like "Delivery Problem" (Graphs) and "Counting" (Combinatorics).

#### Visual & Interactive Learning
*   **Brilliant.org (Discrete Mathematics Path):** Excellent for "learning by doing." It focuses heavily on your "Ask" framework—challenging you to visualize states and connections.
*   **3Blue1Brown (YouTube):** While not a full course, his videos on "Lock Patterns" (Combinatorics) and "Group Theory" provide the best visual intuition for how these abstract concepts work.

#### Specialized Topics
*   **For State Spaces & Graphs:** *“Introduction to Algorithms” (CLRS)*. Chapters on Graph Theory will show you how state spaces are navigated using BFS and DFS.
*   **For Permutations/Combinations:** *“A Walk Through Combinatorics” by Miklós Bóna*. It’s a very friendly introduction to counting.

### Strategy for Learning
If you are starting from scratch, follow this order:
1.  **Sets & Logic** (The foundation)
2.  **Relations & Functions** (How sets interact)
3.  **Combinatorics** (Permutations/Combinations)
4.  **Graph Theory** (Visualizing relations)
5.  **State Machines** (Applying graphs to time/logic)

As you study each, try to answer your "Ask" questions for a real-world scenario (e.g., "How would I represent a Rubik's Cube as a state space? What are the equivalence classes of a deck of cards?").
